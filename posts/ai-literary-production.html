AI, Human Hands, and the Sound of Literature

November 4, 2026

At Mohikontok Sound Lab, we are using artificial intelligence for the up-to-date market — on an experimental basis, and with one rule that never changes: AI generates material, humans decide what moves. This post is about the project that rule is building toward: a large Bengali literary production project, managed as a production from the Bronx studio, whose aim is audio-visual content that can be heard — and therefore reached — by people who cannot see it.

An experimental basis, stated plainly

We call this experimental on purpose. AI is a new instrument in the studio, not a replacement for the artist. It can draft an arrangement, suggest a texture, or help carry a voice across languages — as in our English-to-Bengali dubbing work — but every artistic decision stays human: what to keep, what to cut, what the performance means. That contract between artist and tool is the whole point. Technology extends a heritage its bearers still own; it does not stand in for them.

A production project, not a single recording

This is project management at scale: a catalog of literary works, planned, recorded, produced, and preserved as a body — not one song at a time, but a library built to grow. The studio's room-within-room space, its treated live room, and its cataloging work are the production floor. Each piece is made to a standard it can be found by, listened to, and kept.

Beginning with Bengali folk and traditional composition

The project begins where the Lab's own tradition lives: Bengali folk and traditional composition — the songs, poems, and stories that have traveled by voice for generations. Set in the ambience of modern, cutting-edge technology, these works are recomposed and recorded so that an old song can be heard with new clarity, without losing the hand that made it. From that foundation, the project reaches outward: world literature, carried into Bengali sound — as our Bengali dub of Franz Kafka's The Metamorphosis already shows.

Made to be heard: access for vision-impaired listeners

The aim that shapes every production choice is access. Audio-visual content, made first to be listened to, opens world literature to listeners who are vision-impaired — people who may never read a printed page but can know a story fully through voice, music, and sound. A folk song, a poem recited, a novel dubbed into Bengali: each one is a door into literature that does not ask the listener to see. That is also why the Lab's wider archive work is listen-first — you do not need to read to belong to it.

Human collaboration, at every step

None of this is a machine working alone. It is musicians, reciters, actors, choreographers, and engineers collaborating with human and artificial intelligence — to promote visual art, and to preserve language and culture as curation. The technology is modern. The judgment is human. The audience is everyone, including those who experience the world through sound alone.

This project is still growing, and it is growing in public: new recordings from the Lab, new works added to the catalog, and new listeners finding literature by ear. That is the market we are building for — an up-to-date market, served by an old idea made new: a story, well told, well recorded, and open to all.
